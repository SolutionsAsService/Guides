The lensatic compass

A lensatic compass is built for sighting. You look through a slot at a distant landmark, line it up with a wire, and read the direction through a small magnifying lens without moving the compass away from your eye. That makes it more precise than a flat baseplate compass for taking bearings, at the cost of being harder to use directly on a map.

Cover and sighting wire
Protects the dial when closed. Opened upright, its thin wire is the front sight you line up on a landmark.
Lens and rear sight
The bracket holds a magnifying lens for reading the dial and a slot you look through, like the rear sight on a rifle.
Floating dial
Carries the magnet and spins freely to point at magnetic north. It shows degrees and mils (6,400 to a circle); in this handbook we use degrees.
Fixed index line
A black line on the glass. The number under it is your bearing.
Bezel ring
A notched ring around the glass that clicks in steps of 3° and carries a short luminous line. Use it to set a bearing you want to follow, even in the dark.
Thumb loop
Steadies the compass in your hand. To lock the dial for carrying, fold the lens bracket down over the glass before you close the cover.

Bearings and the three norths

A bearing (also called an azimuth) is a direction given as an angle measured clockwise from north: 0° or 360° is north, 90° east, 180° south and 270° west.

The catch is that there are three norths, and a bearing is only useful if you know which one it is measured from.

True north (TN)
The direction to the North Pole. Map edges run true north–south.
Magnetic north (MN)
Where your compass needle points. It wanders slowly, year by year.
Grid north (GN)
The direction of the vertical grid lines printed on the map. It is within a degree or two of true north on most US maps, so scouts usually treat the two as the same.

The angle between true and magnetic north is the declination. Depending on where you are it can be close to zero or more than 15°, so ignoring it can put you hundreds of metres off over a few kilometres.

Taking a bearing

Use the compass-to-cheek method whenever you need accuracy.

  1. Get clear of metal. Step away from cars, fences and power lines, and hold your phone, knife and metal bottle away from the compass (see accuracy).
  2. Open it. Stand the cover straight up, tilt the lens bracket up to about 45°, and put your thumb through the thumb loop.
  3. Raise it to your eye. Hold the compass level against your cheek, and look through the rear sight slot.
  4. Sight the target. Turn your whole body until the sighting wire lines up with the landmark: a lone tree, a building corner, a summit.
  5. Read the dial. Without moving, glance down through the lens and let the dial settle. The number under the fixed index line is your magnetic bearing.
  6. Write it down, with what you sighted and whether it is magnetic or true, for example "lone pine, 142° M".

For a quick reading that needs to be within about 5°, use the centre-hold method. Open the cover flat, hold the compass level in front of your stomach with both hands, and point the cover at the target.

Following a bearing

In daylight, turn your body until the bearing you want sits under the index line. Then sight along the wire and pick a landmark on that line. Walk to it, and repeat. Choosing landmarks and walking between them lets you go around obstacles without losing the line.

The bezel ring lets you set a bearing in advance, even at night:

  1. Turn the bezel until its luminous line sits directly over the fixed index line. This is your zero.
  2. Divide the bearing by 3 to get the number of clicks. For 81°, that is 27 clicks.
  3. Turn the bezel counterclockwise that many clicks.
  4. Hold the compass in the centre-hold position and turn your body until the north arrow on the dial lines up with the luminous line. You are now facing your bearing.

If your bearing is not a multiple of 3, set the nearest click and correct by eye. A 1° error is about 17 m over a kilometre.

Correcting for declination

Find your declination in the diagram in your map's margin, and check the current value with the NOAA magnetic field calculator, because the printed one may be decades old. Then convert:

  • Magnetic to true (compass → map): add an east declination, subtract a west one.
  • True to magnetic (map → compass): subtract an east declination, add a west one.

A back bearing is the direction back the way you came, or from a landmark to you. Add 180° if the bearing is less than 180°, and subtract 180° if it is 180° or more.

Measuring distance by pacing

Over short distances, use a tape. Everywhere else, count paces. A pace is two steps: count each time your left foot lands.

  1. Measure out 100 m on flat ground with a tape, or use a running track.
  2. Walk it at your normal hiking pace, counting paces. Do it three times and take the average. Most people take 60 to 70 paces.
  3. Write your number inside your compass cover or notebook. That is your pace count.

Your pace shortens uphill, in thick brush, in the dark and when you are carrying a heavy pack, so your count goes up. Practise on a slope to learn by how much. To keep count over long distances, move a bead along a cord or a pebble between pockets every 100 m.

Finding where you are

If you can see landmarks that are on your map, you can find your own position. This is called resection, though many guides call it triangulation.

  1. Orient your map (see Reading a topographic map) and identify two or three landmarks that appear on it, ideally about 60° to 120° apart as seen from you.
  2. Take a bearing to each, and convert each to true.
  3. Work out each back bearing.
  4. With a protractor, draw a line on the map from each landmark along its back bearing. Where the lines cross is your position.
  5. With three landmarks the lines rarely meet at one point. They make a small triangle of error, and you are inside it. A large triangle means a bad reading: take the bearings again.

If you know you are on a linear feature such as a trail, a river or a ridge, one bearing is enough. You are where that back bearing crosses the feature.

Mapping an area

You can map a campsite, a trail or a stretch of creek with nothing more than a compass, your pace count and a notebook. Walk a chain of straight legs between points you choose (called stations). For each leg, write down the bearing and the distance.

  1. Start the sheet. Draw a north arrow and a scale bar, for example 1 cm = 5 m, and mark your starting station.
  2. Plot each leg. Lay a protractor on the station, mark the bearing, and draw a line of the right length to scale.
  3. Add features from your notes, either measured from the nearest station or sketched by eye. Use simple, consistent symbols and include a key.
  4. Close the loop. If your last leg returns to the start, the gap between where it ends and where you began shows your total error. Under about 5% of the distance walked is good work.
  5. Finish the map with a title, the date, your name, the scale and whether north is true or magnetic.

Slopes and heights

A clinometer measures the angle of a slope. Some baseplate compasses have one built in, but a lensatic compass does not, so carry a separate clinometer or make one from a protractor, a straw and a weighted string.

To estimate the height of a tree or cliff: stand a measured distance from its base, sight the top, and read the angle. The height is the distance times the tangent of the angle, plus your eye height. At 30 m and 35°, that is 30 × 0.70 = 21 m, plus about 1.5 m, so roughly 22.5 m. A useful shortcut: at 45°, the height above your eye equals your distance from the base.

Accuracy and safety

Iron, steel and electric currents pull the needle. The US Army's land navigation manual gives safe distances like these:

  • Take every important bearing twice. If two readings differ by more than 2°, take a third and average the two closest.
  • Cross-check with a GPS or phone where you have one, but never rely on a battery to get home.
  • Before you set out, leave your route and return time with someone responsible (see the trip plan).
  • If you become lost: stop, think, observe, plan. Stay put if you cannot work out where you are.

What to bring

  • Lensatic compass
  • Topographic map of the area, in a waterproof bag
  • Notebook and pencil
  • Protractor and ruler
  • Measuring tape, for calibrating your pace
  • Clinometer (optional)
  • GPS or phone for cross-checking (optional)
