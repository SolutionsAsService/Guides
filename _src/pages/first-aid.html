This chapter is a refresher, not training. Take a hands-on course such as American Red Cross First Aid, or a Wilderness First Aid course if your trips go far from roads. In an emergency, call 911, or your local emergency number, as soon as you can.

First steps at any emergency

  1. Check the scene. Is it safe for you to help? Watch for falling rock, traffic, water, fire and lightning. Don't become a second casualty.
  2. Check the person. Are they awake and breathing normally? Is there severe bleeding?
  3. Call for help. Send two people to call 911, or the local emergency number, and to fetch an adult leader. Tell the dispatcher where you are, what happened and how many people are hurt.
  4. Care for the person until help arrives. Keep them still, warm and calm, and keep watching their breathing.

Someone who is not breathing normally needs CPR straight away. CPR has to be learned on a course with a manikin, and every scout old enough should take one.

Bleeding

  1. Put on gloves from the kit if you can.
  2. Press firmly on the wound with a clean dressing or cloth, and keep pressing.
  3. If blood soaks through, add more dressing on top. Don't lift off the first one.
  4. For heavy bleeding that won't stop, keep pressing hard and get emergency help. Tourniquets are for trained people.

Clean small cuts and scrapes with clean running water, then cover them. For a nosebleed, sit up, lean forward, and pinch the soft part of the nose for 10 to 15 minutes.

Burns

  • Cool the burn under cool running water for 20 minutes. Do not use ice, butter or creams.
  • Remove rings, watches and tight clothing near the burn before it swells, but leave anything that is stuck to it.
  • Cover it loosely with a clean, non-fluffy dressing, or with plastic wrap.
  • Get medical help for any burn larger than the person's palm, or on the face, hands, feet, joints or groin, or any burn that looks white, leathery or charred.

Blisters

Stop as soon as you feel a hot spot, a sore or rubbing patch, and cover it with tape or moleskin before it becomes a blister. Check that your socks are dry and your boots are laced snugly.

If a blister forms, leave it intact where you can. Protect it with a ring of moleskin with a hole cut for the blister, and cover it with a dressing. If it breaks, wash it, leave the skin flap in place, and cover it.

Sprains and strains

For a twisted ankle or knee, rest it, put a cold pack on it wrapped in cloth (never ice straight on skin), support it, and raise it. Get medical help if the person can't put any weight on it, if it looks misshapen, or if the pain is severe. Treat it as a possible fracture: don't try to straighten it.

Heat illness

Heat exhaustion
Heavy sweating, cool, pale and clammy skin, a fast weak pulse, nausea or vomiting, cramps, tiredness, dizziness or headache. Move them to shade, loosen clothing, cool them with wet cloths, and have them sip water. Get medical help if they vomit, or if they get worse or are not better within an hour.
Heat stroke: an emergency
A high body temperature, hot red skin that may be dry or damp, a fast strong pulse, headache, and confusion or collapse. Call 911 at once. Move them somewhere cooler and cool them fast with cool water and wet cloths, or in a cool bath. Do not give them anything to drink.

Prevent both: drink regularly, rest in shade, plan hard work for the cool of the morning, and watch each other. Based on CDC heat guidance.

Hypothermia and frostbite

Hypothermia happens when the body loses heat faster than it makes it. It is most common in cool, wet and windy weather, not just in deep cold. Watch for the "umbles": someone who stumbles, mumbles, fumbles and grumbles, plus shivering, drowsiness and confusion.

  1. Get them out of the wind and wet, into a tent or shelter.
  2. Replace wet clothing with dry clothing, and insulate them from the ground.
  3. Warm the centre of the body first: wrap them in a sleeping bag, with warm (not hot) water bottles at the chest and neck.
  4. If they are fully alert, give warm, sweet drinks and food.
  5. Call for help if they stop shivering, become confused or very drowsy, or can't be warmed. Handle them gently.

Frostbite makes skin white or greyish, hard or waxy, and numb, usually on fingers, toes, nose or ears. Get the person somewhere warm and don't rub the area. Rewarm it only if it won't freeze again, and get medical help. See the CDC's winter weather guidance.

Ticks and stings

Check yourself and each other for ticks every day, especially behind the knees, at the waist, under the arms, along the hairline and behind the ears. To remove a tick:

  1. Grasp it as close to the skin as possible with clean, fine-tipped tweezers.
  2. Pull straight away from the skin with steady, even pressure. Don't twist or jerk.
  3. Clean the bite and your hands with soap and water, alcohol or hand sanitiser.
  4. Don't crush the tick. Seal it in a bag or tape. Don't use heat, petroleum jelly or nail polish to make it let go.

Note the date and see a doctor if a rash or fever develops in the following weeks. Steps from the CDC.

For a bee sting, scrape the stinger out sideways with a fingernail or card, then cool the spot. Anyone with swelling of the face or throat, wheezing or difficulty breathing after a sting needs emergency help at once. If they carry an epinephrine auto-injector, help them use it.

The group first-aid kit

  • Disposable gloves
  • Adhesive bandages, several sizes
  • Sterile gauze pads and roller bandage
  • Adhesive tape
  • Moleskin or blister dressings
  • Elastic bandage
  • Triangular bandage
  • Tweezers
  • Small scissors
  • Antiseptic wipes
  • Instant cold pack
  • Emergency blanket
  • CPR face shield
  • Pencil, paper and this page

Each person also carries their own medicines, and the leader carries health forms and knows about allergies. Check the kit before every trip and replace anything used or out of date.
