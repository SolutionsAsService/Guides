The Ten Essentials

The Ten Essentials began with The Mountaineers in Seattle in the 1930s and are now grouped as systems rather than single items. Carry all ten on every outing, even a short day hike. They are what lets you spend an unplanned night out safely.

  1. NavigationMap, compass and the skill to use them, plus a phone or GPS as backup.
  2. HeadlampWith spare batteries. Hikes run late more often than anyone plans.
  3. Sun protectionSunglasses, sunscreen, a hat, and clothing that covers you.
  4. First aidA kit sized for your group and trip, and someone who knows how to use it. See the first-aid kit.
  5. KnifeOr a multi-tool, for repairs, first aid and food.
  6. FireWaterproof matches or a lighter, and a firestarter.
  7. Emergency shelterA space blanket, emergency bivvy or tarp, even on a day hike.
  8. Extra foodAt least a day's worth beyond what you plan to eat.
  9. Extra waterAnd a way to treat more. See safe water.
  10. Extra clothesEnough to survive the worst weather you could meet, not the forecast.

Adapted from The Mountaineers' Ten Essentials.

The trip plan

Before every outing, write down where you are going and leave it with a responsible adult who is not coming. If you are late, they know where to send help and when.

Leave behind

Who
Everyone in the group, with an emergency contact for each.
Where
Trailhead, route, planned campsites, and any alternatives you might take.
When
Start time, expected return, and the time to call for help if you haven't checked in.
Vehicles
Make, colour and licence plate, and where they are parked.
Gear
Tent and pack colours, and whether you have a phone, radio or satellite messenger.

Check the weather forecast and any closures, fire restrictions or permit rules the day before you go. Once you have left the plan, stick to it, or tell your contact before you change it.

Dressing in layers

Several thin layers keep you more comfortable than one thick one, because you can add or remove them as you warm up and cool down.

Base layer
Next to your skin. Synthetic or wool fabric moves sweat away from you.
Insulating layer
Fleece, wool or a puffy jacket, to trap warmth.
Shell
A waterproof, windproof jacket and trousers.

Avoid cotton, including jeans and cotton T-shirts. It soaks up water and sweat, then stays wet and pulls heat out of your body, which can lead to hypothermia even in mild weather. Take a layer off before you start to sweat, and put one on as soon as you stop.

Safe water

Treat all water from streams, lakes and springs, however clear it looks: it can carry germs that make you ill days later. Use one of these methods:

Boil
Bring clear water to a rolling boil for 1 minute, or 3 minutes above 6,500 feet (about 2,000 m). This is the most reliable method.
Filter
Use a filter made for backcountry water, and follow its instructions. Most filters do not remove viruses.
Chemical treatment
Use tablets or drops made for drinking water, and wait the full contact time on the label. Cold or cloudy water needs longer.

If water is cloudy, let it settle or strain it through a clean cloth first. Neither boiling nor filtering makes water safe from fuel or chemical contamination, so collect from somewhere else. Guidance from the CDC.

Packing a day pack

  • Heavy items close to your back and in the middle. Things you need during the day, like water, snacks, a map and a rain jacket, go where you can reach them without unpacking.
  • Line your pack with a strong plastic bag, or put clothes and your sleeping bag in dry bags. Pack covers alone leak.
  • Carry only what the day needs, and weigh your loaded pack before you leave. A lighter pack is safer on rough ground, and less tiring.
