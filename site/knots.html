Knots and rope

Words for rope

Knot instructions use a few words for the parts of a rope. Learn these first and every set of steps gets easier.

Working end
The end you move to tie the knot.
Standing part
The rest of the rope, which you don't move.
Bight
A U-shaped fold in the rope that does not cross itself.
Loop
A circle where the rope crosses over itself.
Hitch
A knot that ties a rope to something else, such as a post, a ring or another rope.

Square knot

Use it for: tying the two ends of one rope or cord around something, such as a bundle or a bandage.

  1. Take an end in each hand. Cross the right end over the left, and tuck it under and up.
  2. Now cross the end in your left hand over the one in your right, and tuck it under and up.
  3. Pull both ends tight. Remember: right over left, left over right.

Check it: each end lies alongside its own standing part, and the knot looks flat and even. If the ends stick out across the knot, you have tied a granny knot, which slips. Untie it and start again.

Never use a square knot to join two ropes that will carry a load. It can shake loose or capsize. Use a sheet bend instead.

Bowline

Use it for: a fixed loop at the end of a rope that won't slip or tighten, for example around a tree, a post or a person in a rescue.

  1. Make a small loop in the standing part, with the working end's side on top. This is the rabbit hole, and the standing part above it is the tree.
  2. Leave enough rope below the loop for the size of loop you want. Bring the working end (the rabbit) up through the small loop from underneath.
  3. Take it around behind the standing part (around the tree).
  4. Bring it back down through the small loop (down the hole).
  5. Hold the working end against the big loop, and pull the standing part to tighten.

Check it: the working end finishes inside the big loop. Leave a tail at least ten times the rope's thickness.

Two half hitches

Use it for: tying a rope to a post, ring or tree when you want to untie it easily later, such as a tarp ridgeline or a boat's painter.

  1. Pass the working end around the post.
  2. Bring it over the standing part, then around and through the gap you have just made. That is one half hitch.
  3. Make a second half hitch the same way, further along the standing part.
  4. Slide both hitches up snug against the post.

Check it: together the two hitches look like a clove hitch tied around the standing part.

Watch it tied (Animated Knots)

Clove hitch

Use it for: starting and finishing lashings, and quickly tying a line to a pole.

  1. Wrap the working end once around the pole.
  2. Cross it over its own standing part, and wrap around the pole a second time.
  3. Tuck the working end under the second wrap, so it comes out between the two wraps and opposite the standing part.
  4. Pull both ends to tighten.

Check it: from the front you see an X, and the two ends leave in opposite directions from under it.

A clove hitch can work loose if the pole turns or the load keeps changing. For anything important, add a half hitch around the standing part.

Watch it tied (Animated Knots)

Taut-line hitch

Use it for: tent guy lines and clotheslines. It slides to tighten or loosen the line, and grips when loaded.

  1. Pass the working end around the stake, and bring it back alongside the standing part.
  2. Wrap it twice around the standing part, working toward the stake and inside the loop you have made.
  3. Bring the working end back across the first two wraps, and make one more wrap around the standing part outside the loop, on the side away from the stake.
  4. Tuck the working end through that last wrap and pull it snug.
  5. Slide the knot away from the stake to tighten the line.

Check it: the knot slides when you push it, but holds when the line is pulled.

See the closely related midshipman's hitch (Animated Knots)

Sheet bend

Use it for: joining two ropes, especially when one is thicker than the other.

  1. Make a bight in the end of the thicker rope, and hold it.
  2. Pass the end of the thinner rope up through the bight from underneath.
  3. Take it around behind both parts of the bight.
  4. Tuck it under itself, not under the bight, so it lies across the bight's neck.
  5. Hold the bight and pull the thin rope's standing part to tighten.

Check it: both short ends finish on the same side of the knot. If they are on opposite sides, you have tied a left-handed sheet bend, which is weaker. Tie it again.

Watch it tied (Animated Knots)

Timber hitch

Use it for: dragging or lifting a log, and starting a diagonal lashing. It holds firmly under load and falls apart easily when the load comes off.

  1. Pass the working end around the log.
  2. Take it around the standing part.
  3. Twist the working end around its own part of the loop, three or more times.
  4. Pull the standing part so the loop tightens around the log.

Check it: the twists lie snug against the log. To drag a long log straight, tie the timber hitch near the back end, then add a half hitch around the log near the front end, the end you pull from.

Caring for rope

  • Stop the ends fraying. Melt the end of synthetic rope with a flame, being careful because the molten plastic burns. Whip the end of natural-fibre rope by binding it tightly with thin twine.
  • Keep it clean and dry. Don't step on rope, which grinds grit into the fibres. Dry wet rope before you store it.
  • Coil it in even loops, and finish with a few wraps and a hitch so it doesn't tangle.
  • Retire it if it is cut, worn, stiff, glazed by heat or badly faded. Never use camp rope for climbing.
